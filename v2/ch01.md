# Chapter 1: The Promise of Provable and Programmable Secrets

## The Trick

You know something and I need to check it. Conventional verification runs on disclosure. You open the books and you show the document and you reveal the source code and you hand over the key, and every one of those is the same move made with different paperwork. Knowledge flows from the one who has it to the one who wants it, and some of it always spills on the way.

To prove is to show; to show is to reveal. Every check we have built asks for the record rather than the fact, and it has been that way long enough to look like a law of nature rather than a design choice.

To verify one fact about a person you first assemble a record of that person. The record must exist before the verification can happen, and once it exists it can be copied and sold and subpoenaed and stolen. The dossier is the method.

Then in 1985 Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali and Charles Rackoff showed that you can prove a statement is true while revealing nothing about why it is true [GMR: STOC 1985, pp. 291–304; journal version SICOMP 18(1):186–208, 1989]. The nothing is exact, and it is nothing in a sense that can be written down as a theorem and checked by anyone who cares to. The proof convinces. It doesn't inform.

The three things it promises looked incompatible, and the paper set out to overturn exactly that. A proof that convinces must carry information. Otherwise what is the verifier checking? The way through is that interaction and randomness can stand in for disclosure, so that a pattern of answers to unpredictable questions is convincing while each answer on its own means nothing.

Zero-knowledge proofs do not eliminate trust. They decompose it. They take one monolithic act of faith, the kind that says trust the bank, or trust the platform, and they spread it across seven layers. The seven are setup, language, witness, arithmetization, proof system, primitives and verification. The layers are not themselves the assumptions. They are where the assumptions live, and one layer can hold several, and some hold no assumption at all but a procedure: a ceremony, a compiler, a machine, a committee. After this chapter the book takes one layer to a chapter, starting with the ceremony in Chapter 2 and ending with the contract that checks the result. Each one can be named and examined on its own. Each one is replaceable, at least in principle, and that qualification is going to cost this book several chapters, because the layers are coupled and pulling on one of them moves the others. What is left when the mathematics has finished its work is less trust than you started with. It is spread over more places too. Every one of those places can be audited on its own terms by somebody who doesn't believe you.

The word "trustless" is marketing. The accurate word is "trust-minimized." It's uglier and it's correct.

Consider what trust looks like now. At the door of a bar you hand your identity documents to a stranger. He needs one bit, old enough or not. He receives a dossier instead, and he doesn't want the dossier and he has no use for most of it. But he takes it anyway, because handing over the whole card has always been the only way anyone knew to get the one bit out of it.

Zero-knowledge proofs make it possible to send the bit and keep the dossier.

Two characters drive the story. One of them knows a secret and wants to convince somebody of a fact without surrendering the secret. The other checks the work and returns a verdict, accept or reject. The literature calls them the prover and the verifier. Every zero-knowledge system ever built reduces to that pair, and the rollups holding billions of dollars and the identity credential sitting in a phone come down in the end to two characters and one verdict.

Neither of them has to like the other and neither of them has to be honest, and the whole subject exists because the verifier is allowed to assume the prover is lying and the prover is allowed to assume that anything she hands over she will never get back. The cleanest way to understand the pair is to be one of them. Here is a puzzle. You are the verifier.

## Play the Game

```
+---+---+---+---+
| 1 |   |   | 4 |
+---+---+---+---+
|   | 4 | 1 |   |
+---+---+---+---+
|   | 1 |   |   |
+---+---+---+---+
| 4 |   |   | 1 |
+---+---+---+---+
```

Seven cells are given and nine are blank. Each row, each column and each 2x2 box holds 1, 2, 3 and 4 exactly once. That is the whole rule. There's nothing else to know. The grid is small and the rule is short and you can hold them both in your head at the same time.

A woman across the table says she holds a completed grid. It agrees with every given cell. It breaks no rule. She won't show it to you and she isn't going to change her mind about that.

Be careful about what she is claiming. This puzzle has more than one completion, so she isn't claiming that some particular grid is the answer. She is claiming that she is holding one of them.

You are not going to check her arithmetic. You are going to test her, over and over, in a way she can't prepare for. At the end of it you will believe her or you won't.

She has sixteen envelopes on the table beside her, one for every cell of the grid. She will use all sixteen every round even though most of them are never going to be opened. Into each envelope she puts the value her grid has in that cell, and she seals them and lays them face down in four rows of four. She does all of this before you say anything at all.

The sealing is a commitment, and the commitment is what makes the protocol work. She has to fix sixteen values before she learns what you're going to ask, and once the envelopes are on the table she can't change what's inside them.

Think about what she could do if the order ran the other way, if the challenge came first and the sealing came second. That is the version of this game that falls apart. If you named a line first and she filled the envelopes second, she would fill that one line in correctly and put whatever she liked in the other twelve cells. She would pass every round of this game forever without ever holding a solution. The whole strength of the protocol is in the sequence. She commits first and you challenge second and she opens third, and no other order of those three does any work at all.

Now you name a line. A line is any row, any column or any 2x2 box. That gives you twelve to choose from, four rows and four columns and four boxes. You draw yours at random from the twelve and you draw it where she can't see it coming, and that requirement is as much a part of the protocol as her sealing, because a verifier with a habit is a verifier a bluffer can plan around. She picks up the envelopes belonging to the cells of that line that were blank in the puzzle and she leaves the other envelopes lying where they are, and she opens them in front of you, in an order she chooses at random and she lays the values down as they come. The given cells are printed on the puzzle already. They stay where they are. Nobody has to open anything to find out what they say. You look at what she opened and you add the givens from that line and you check that you are looking at 1, 2, 3 and 4, each of them once. If it comes out any other way you stop and you refuse her. There is no second chance inside a round.

Then she sweeps the table. She seals sixteen new envelopes from the same grid and lays them face down again. Only after they are down do you choose the next line. The envelopes are fresh every round. If she kept one committed grid on the table and you opened a different line each round, you would own the whole grid by the end of the afternoon. The game would be a slow way of asking her to show it to you.

So what does a single round buy you? Not much on its own. One round tests one line out of twelve and leaves eleven untested; a woman with a bad grid gets past it at most eleven times in twelve. The game is built out of a great many weak tests. The weakness is what keeps each one cheap enough to repeat.

Play three rounds. These first three are picked for you, one of each shape, so that you can see what each shape asks. After that you flip for it. You go first. She waits.

You name the third row. In the puzzle that row reads blank, 1, blank, blank. She has three envelopes to open, and she opens them in the order she likes. The first comes up 4. The second comes up 2. The third comes up 3. Together with the 1 already printed in the puzzle that is 1, 2, 3 and 4, each of them exactly once and the row is clean. What you don't know is which cell held which value, because she opened them shuffled, so the 4 you saw first could have come out of the first column or the third or the fourth and the order she chose tells you nothing at all about the grid. Thirteen of the sixteen envelopes were never touched.

She sweeps the table and seals sixteen more.

Then you name the first column. In the puzzle it reads 1, blank, blank, 4, so she has two envelopes this time. The first comes up 2 and the second comes up 3 and with the givens 1 and 4 the column holds all four values once each. What stays hidden is whether that 3 sat in row two or row three, and that one missing fact is enough to keep the column from you, because a column whose values you know and whose order you do not know is a column you cannot fill in. You have seen the values. You haven't seen the positions, and the positions are where the solution lives.

She sweeps the table again.

Then you name the bottom-right box, the four cells at rows three and four, columns three and four. That box holds one given, the 1 in the corner, and three blanks. She opens three envelopes. The first is 2, the second is 4, the third is 3. With the given 1 that is all four values, once each. Everything about position stays hidden, all six permutations of those three values across those three cells, and you have no reason to prefer any one of the six.

The box was a different shape of test from the row and the column. A box is a constraint that the rows and the columns between them cannot see. A grid can have four clean rows and four clean columns and still be wrong inside a box, and a bluffer who only had to survive rows and columns would have a much easier afternoon. A game that only ever asked about rows would be checking a third of what the rules require. It would be quoting a number it had not earned. Twelve lines is the whole rulebook. Every constraint the puzzle imposes is one of the twelve and every one of the twelve is a question you are allowed to ask.

By now you may have noticed something, and it deserves an honest answer rather than a change of subject. You could have written down the values she was going to open before she sealed a single envelope. The puzzle is public. Take any line and look at the givens sitting in it and subtract them from 1, 2, 3 and 4, and what is left is exactly what she will show you, and rounds one and three each had a single given, a 1, and each came back 2, 3 and 4 in some order. Round two had two givens and came back 2 and 3. The values were never in doubt. Only the order was. She chose it at random. We will come back to it.

Now consider a woman who has nothing.

She has no completed grid. She never had one and she is going to bluff. But she still has to fill sixteen envelopes and seal them before she hears which line you will name, and whatever she puts inside them, at least one of the twelve lines comes out wrong, because if all twelve came out right she would be holding a solution after all.

The twelve lines are the entire constraint system. A grid that satisfies all twelve is a solved grid by definition and not by luck, and a woman who cannot produce a solved grid cannot produce sixteen values that satisfy all twelve lines. Whatever she seals, something in there is broken and the broken thing is sitting in at least one of the twelve places you are about to choose from.

So there is at least one line out of twelve that catches her. You name a line at random. She gets through the round with probability at most eleven twelfths, which is about 0.92. That number is a bound and not a forecast, and the difference matters. On this puzzle no false grid breaks only one line. Put a wrong value in a cell and you have wounded its row and its column at once, and often its box as well, so a real bluffer is losing two of the twelve or more. Eleven twelfths is the most conservative figure anybody can write down. It is what a perfect bluffer could not beat if a perfect bluffer existed here, and none does. That is exactly why it is the number worth quoting. Everything the real world can put in front of you does worse.

Then she has to seal again. Whatever she got away with last round is swept off the table and gone, and she faces a fresh sixteen with at least one bad line still in it and a draw she still cannot see coming, so this round is at most eleven twelfths all over again no matter what happened before it, and the per-round bounds multiply. Two rounds leave her at most about 0.84. Eight rounds leave her at most about 0.50. Eight rounds of this game are a coin flip. You shouldn't accept eight. Sixteen rounds leave at most about 0.25. Fifty rounds leave at most about 0.013, roughly one chance in 78. A hundred rounds leave at most about one chance in 6,000. Two hundred rounds leave at most about one chance in 36 million.

Practitioners stop counting at 2^-40. It is a convention and not a theorem, and it says how small a bluffer's chance has to be before a working cryptographer stops arguing. The 128-bit figure later in this chapter prices an attacker's work instead, and it is a different quantity. It means an attacker needs about 2^128 operations to break the thing, and no machine anybody will ever build can run that many.

Three hundred and nineteen rounds put her survival at no more than one chance in 1.1 trillion. That is below 2^-40, two to the minus fortieth power, the line this book will treat as gone. Run the game 318 times and she is still above the line. Run it 319 times and she is under it. The difference between those two numbers is one more round.

That is a long afternoon with envelopes. Nobody standing at a door has an afternoon.

Here is what you have after all 319 rounds. She is holding one of the completions of this puzzle. You do not know which one. Every value she opened all afternoon was a value you could have written down before she sat down, and the only thing you learned that you did not walk in with is that a solution exists and that it is in her hands.

You can build this game yourself in about two hundred lines of Python: sixteen commitments, a random draw from the twelve, and an open-and-check on the line that came up. Then cheat with it. Seal any grid that isn't a solution and count the rounds until the check catches you, and do it a few dozen times and see where the counts pile up. The bound assumes exactly one broken line, and a real bad cell breaks two lines or more, so the counts come in low. The bound predicts about twelve rounds to a catch, because a test that succeeds one time in twelve takes about twelve tries. Your counts will pile up well short of that. Break two lines instead of one and the test catches her one time in six, so the expected count halves to six. That gap between what the bound promised and what the table shows you is the slack in the bound, and you can measure it in an afternoon. Reading the arithmetic isn't the same as watching it happen.

## The Proof at the Door

You are twenty-two years old. You walk into a bar. The bouncer asks for your ID and you reach into your wallet and hand over a government card. It carries your full legal name, your date of birth, your home address, your photograph and your driver's license number, and depending on where you live it also carries your organ donor status and a corrective lens restriction. He glances at the birth year, works out that you clear the line and hands the card back across the bar. He does not look at any of the rest of it. None of the rest of it is his business.

Five pieces of personally identifiable information, and two more depending on where you live, disclosed in three seconds to answer one bit.

The card was designed in an era when the only way to prove a fact about yourself was to hand over the document containing the fact. We've lived with that design long enough that it feels like the shape of the world. It is only an engineering decision about what would fit on a piece of card.

Now imagine the other exchange. You carry a digital credential on your phone, issued by the same authority and carrying the same legal weight. The terminal asks whether you are 21 or older. Your phone computes for a fraction of a second and sends back a proof and a green light comes on.

That exchange is the 319 rounds you played, with the envelopes hidden inside a phone. The credential is her grid. The terminal's challenge is the line you drew from the twelve. The proof is what she opened. Everything you learned at the table applies at the door, and the three things that made the game work have names.

The first is **completeness** and it's the promise made to the honest prover. If she really holds a completion, then every line you can name is a line her grid satisfies, so every envelope she opens checks out, in round one and in round 319 and in every round between. She is accepted with probability exactly 1. There's no unlucky round for an honest prover and no edge case where a valid grid fails a valid check.

At the door, be precise about what that covers. Completeness is a promise about the protocol and not about the phone or the terminal or the network standing behind them. Run the specified protocol on a valid credential and the check accepts, every time, and nothing in that sentence stops the terminal from being down. What completeness buys is that the mathematics is never the thing that turns the twenty-two-year-old away. A system that misses even that dies on contact with the world, and the people it fails are the people who were using it correctly, and they're the ones who will tell everybody.

The second is **soundness**, and it's the promise made to you. It is quoted as a soundness error rather than as a guarantee, and the error has a name, ε. In one round of the Sudoku game ε is at most eleven twelfths, and after n rounds it is at most eleven twelfths raised to the nth power. At 319 rounds it is below 2^-40.

Soundness doesn't promise that cheating is impossible; it prices it. The soundness error is that price, and it is charged against every strategy she might play. However she fills the envelopes, if what she sealed is not a solution she survives a round with probability at most eleven twelfths. You pay the price down in rounds.

Notice also what you were during all of this. You were cheap. You flipped a coin and you read four numbers and you said yes or no, and you did it in bounded time without ever solving the puzzle yourself. A verifier that is efficient and randomized is what makes any of this usable at a door and that probabilistic game between a prover and an efficient randomized verifier is exactly what Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff defined.

The third is **zero-knowledge**, and it's the promise made to her. It is a claim about what somebody holding nothing could produce, not a claim about how little the messages contain.

Take a clerk with a pencil and the printed puzzle. She has no completion of it. She never had one and she isn't going to get one, and she is not trying to fool anybody, and there is nobody in the room with her while she works. Here is her whole procedure.

She picks a line at random from the twelve. She looks at the givens sitting in that line and works out which of the values 1, 2, 3 and 4 are missing from it, which is arithmetic on public information and takes her about a second. She writes those values down in a random order. She lays sixteen sealed envelopes down beside them, and all but the two or three that line calls for she will never open and never had to fill, because a sealed envelope shows nothing and showing nothing is the only thing anybody needs a sealed envelope to do. She calls that a round. Then she does it again with another line and another order, and she keeps going until she has 319 of them and she hands you the stack.

What she hands you is drawn from exactly the same distribution as the transcript of a real game. The two are identical. A transcript never shows anything except a shuffle of the values the puzzle already told you were missing, sitting beside a pile of envelopes nobody opened, and shuffling is something a woman with no grid can do exactly as well as a woman with one.

Notice what separates the clerk from the bluffer of the last section. They look alike and they are not alike at all. The bluffer had to answer a challenge she couldn't predict, and that is why she lost, eleven twelfths at a time, all afternoon. The clerk picks the challenge herself. That is why she never fails, and it is also why her transcript of 319 rounds convinces nobody of anything. She has proved nothing. She has only shown that the transcript of a proof and the transcript of nothing are the same object.

That is **perfect** zero-knowledge against a verifier who draws his line honestly, and both halves of that phrase are load-bearing. Take the perfect part first. Two weaker words exist and both of them get used. Statistical zero-knowledge would allow the two distributions to differ by a vanishing amount. Computational zero-knowledge would allow them to differ by an amount that no efficient test can find. The game on the table gives you the strongest of the three, and it gives it to you because the envelopes are made of paper. Paper hides perfectly. Nothing you can do to a sealed envelope with your eyes will tell you what is inside it.

At the door the envelope is a commitment scheme running on a chip, and a sealed envelope was quietly doing two jobs at once. Hiding is the first: you cannot learn the value inside. Binding is the second: she cannot change the value after the seal. Paper does both perfectly and for free, because paper is a fiction. No real scheme is perfectly hiding and perfectly binding at the same time. Arithmetic has to choose. The schemes behind the systems in this book keep their binding and give up perfect hiding. No machine anybody can build will let her change a sealed value. What gives is the hiding, because the committed value stays secret against anybody with a realistic amount of computing power, which is a different sentence from stays secret against anybody at all, and it comes with a different warranty behind it. Hiding is the guarantee zero-knowledge rests on. That is why almost every system in this book settles for the computational version of the property rather than the perfect one. The property did not change. The envelope did.

Now the second half of the phrase. It will matter in a page. The clerk drew her line at random, the way an honest verifier draws. A verifier who studies the sixteen envelopes for a while and then decides which line to name is a different opponent, and this clerk cannot stand in for him, because she chose her line before she knew what she was going to lay down beside it. What the game gives you is zero-knowledge against a verifier who draws honestly, and covering the other kind costs more, and this book pays for it later.

So zero-knowledge is a property of the verifier's view. It is not a property of the messages. The messages were never empty, and every value she opened was a real value out of a real grid that she really holds. What makes them tell you nothing is that a clerk holding no grid at all produces messages drawn from the identical distribution. At the door, an adversary who intercepts your credential proof learns what the bouncer learned and stops there, because the transcript he captured could have been manufactured by somebody who's never met you.

There is a fourth promise, and it closes the set. Back at the table she was careful about what she claimed. She never said which completion was the answer. She said she was holding one. Knowledge-soundness is the property that makes that sentence mean something, and it asks more than that cheats fail. It asks for extraction, which means that anybody who convinces you must be holding a grid. The extractor is not a verifier, and he is allowed what no verifier is allowed. He can run her again from the same sealed sixteen and name a different line, and again, and after enough reruns the lines she opens cover the grid and he assembles it. Nobody at the table can rewind her that way, so this is not her showing the grid to anybody. The property is defined by the existence of that extractor rather than by any promise she makes, and that is where the K in SNARK comes from, which is short for Succinct Non-interactive ARgument of Knowledge.

So take the three in turn and ask the same thing of each. What does it insure, and who is holding the loss when it fails?

Soundness insures the verifier against a lying prover. When it fails the verifier eats it. The bar serves someone it shouldn't have served, and the bar is what gets fined; the nineteen-year-old walks out into the night with a drink in him. Losses from a soundness failure land on the party that accepted the proof, which is why the party that accepts proofs is usually the party willing to pay for more rounds.

Zero-knowledge insures the prover against an extractive verifier. When it fails the prover eats it and this loss has a property that the other two don't have. It can't be reversed. A wrongly served drink can be refused the next time and a wrongly turned-away customer can come back tomorrow, but a date of birth that has left your phone is out and it stays out, and there's no version of the world where you get it back.

Completeness insures the honest party against the system itself. When it fails the honest prover eats it and she usually can't demonstrate that she was wronged. A rejection looks the same from the outside whether the system was right about her or simply broken. There's nobody to sue and nothing to point at. That is why completeness is the property most often left out of a threat model, and it is also why the failures that quietly kill a deployment are so often completeness failures. They are rarely the dramatic kind.

None of the three is free. Each of them is bought, with rounds or with an assumption. One of them can also be bought on credit.

## Firing the Verifier

The trouble with 319 rounds is that they need a live verifier for all 319 of them, present and paying attention. Both parties have to be awake at the same time and pointed at the same table, and neither can leave until it is finished. Worse, when it is over there is nothing to keep. The proof existed in the exchange and only in the exchange. If a second bouncer wants convincing tomorrow, the whole afternoon has to happen again from the first envelope.

That's a strange thing to own. It convinced one man, once. It convinced him only because he was standing there while it happened. Nothing about it can be filed or forwarded or put in front of a court, and nothing of it survives the moment the two of them stand up. A proof that can't be handed to a third party is not much use to an institution, whatever it does for two people at a table.

What did the verifier actually do? He picked a number from one to twelve at random, and he picked it honestly, and he brought no knowledge to it beyond that, no judgment and no skill. The protocol never asked him for any, and it never had to. And that is the entire job.

In 1986 Amos Fiat and Adi Shamir noticed that a job that small can be done by a function [Fiat and Shamir, Crypto 1986; LNCS 263, pp. 186–194, 1987]. They fired the verifier and hired a cryptographic hash.

The move only works on a particular kind of protocol. A protocol is public-coin when every message the verifier sends is nothing but its coin flips, published as they fall, with no private state sitting behind them, and a hash can reproduce a coin flip because a coin flip depends on nothing that came before it and on nothing the verifier ever knew. But a hash can't reproduce a message that depended on something the verifier knew and kept to himself, because the hash was never told what he knew. Our Sudoku game is public-coin. "Name a line, one through twelve" is a coin and nothing else, and that is the only reason the game can be transformed at all.

Not every protocol is built that way. A verifier that holds a secret and sends messages shaped by that secret cannot be replaced by a public function, because the function would have to know the secret, and if it knew the secret it wouldn't be public. The public-coin condition is what makes the verifier replaceable. A verifier who does nothing but flip coins in the open is a verifier you can automate.

The mechanism has to be stated carefully, because the obvious version of it is broken. Suppose she hashed one round at a time. She seals sixteen commitments, hashes them, reads her line out of the digest, and if the digest names a line she can't survive she reseals and hashes again until it names one she can. Ask what that costs the woman we have been pricing all afternoon, the one with a single bad line in the grid. Eleven digests in twelve already name a line she is fine with. She expects to hash about one and a tenth times per round and then walk on. Three hundred and nineteen rounds cost her something in the region of three hundred and fifty hashes, which is a few seconds of a laptop's attention, and she wins every round. Not most of them. Every one of them. Round-by-round hashing does not weaken the amplification. It deletes it, and it does so cheaply enough that she could do it while the kettle boils.

So the transform doesn't run round by round. Every round's commitments go down first, the whole afternoon's tables at once, and then one hash of everything, the puzzle and every commitment on it, issues all the challenges together, one line named in each round. In the interactive game she sealed a fresh sixteen every round and never saw the next line coming. Here she commits to the entire afternoon before the hash speaks, and if she doesn't like what it says her only move is to reseal every envelope on the table and start the whole thing again.

That doesn't make regrinding impossible. It makes it expensive, and expensive is a number somebody has to write down. Each complete re-seal is one more attempt at the whole afternoon, and each attempt succeeds with probability at most eleven twelfths to the nth power. Her chance overall is the number of attempts she can afford multiplied by that per-attempt probability. A live verifier gave her one draw and no more. The hash gives her as many draws as she can afford to compute. Price her at billions of complete tables, which is a forger with a warehouse and a month rather than a woman with envelopes, and 319 rounds stops buying what it bought across the table: billions of attempted afternoons against a one-in-a-trillion afternoon leave her odds at something no careful person would sign. To put her back under 2^-40 against that forger the afternoon has to grow to roughly 560 rounds, and every one of those rounds is sixteen more envelopes sealed before the hash is allowed to speak. That is what the transform charges, and the charge is not optional. The 319 was priced against a man who could ask once.

None of this works unless every challenge is a coin flip and nothing else. That is the public-coin condition, and it is what makes the verifier replaceable, but a second condition stands beside it and is not the same condition. The hash has to eat the whole table, every public value and every commitment on it, because a challenge derived from part of the table is a challenge she can steer toward the part you left out. And a challenge she can steer is no challenge at all. The whole-table hash also pays an older debt. Zero-knowledge at the table held only against a verifier who drew his line honestly, and the hash draws exactly that way, blindly and from the whole table, so the honest-verifier restriction is the one the transform needs, and it survives the move.

What comes out the far end is a non-interactive argument. One file, computed alone at whatever hour suits her and verifiable by anybody at any time from now on. The word "argument" is doing work there, and the concession is worth naming. A proof holds against any prover at all. An argument holds against a prover with bounded computing power, and every system in this book is an argument.

What did they get for the trade? They got a file. A file sounds small next to an afternoon of envelopes. That is exactly the difference between a demonstration and an artifact. The interactive proof was an event and it ended when the two of them stood up. The transformed one is an object, and objects can be stored and copied and posted to a chain and checked by somebody who was not born when it was made.

The security theorem holds in the random-oracle model. You model the hash as an oracle that answers every fresh query with a uniform value nobody can predict without asking for it, and in that model the transformed argument is sound. There is one real cost visible inside the model, and you have already met it. A prover who can query the oracle many times gets that many attempts at a favorable challenge sequence instead of the single attempt a live verifier would have allowed her, so the error an honest analysis has to quote is roughly her query budget multiplied by the error of the interactive game. That multiplication is the arithmetic that took the afternoon from 319 rounds to 560.

Then the oracle gets instantiated with a real hash. SHA-256 is not an oracle. It's a fixed public function whose code you can read, whose outputs you can compute for yourself and whose entire behavior was settled before anybody asked it a question. The theorem was proved about an object that SHA-256 is not, and no theorem anywhere carries the guarantee across that instantiation. What can be said honestly is narrower than the reassurance people usually reach for and more useful than it. No theorem covers the swap, and what stands in place of one is a long record of people attacking real hash functions in this job and coming away with nothing. The Fiat-Shamir failures this book is going to show you are not failures of that substitution, and not one of them is somebody finding the gap between the hash and the oracle and walking through it. They are failures of what gets fed to the hash, and nobody ever proved a theorem about that.

There is a second cost, and it is subtler than the assumption.

The transcript is the contract between the two parties. A live verifier drew his line only after the whole table was down, so there was nothing on that table he could have failed to account for, whether or not anybody had thought carefully about which parts mattered. A hash depends on what you feed it and on nothing else. Leave one public input out of the hash input, or one commitment, and the prover can work the whole thing backwards from the challenge she wants, and she computes the challenge the digest will produce and then chooses the omitted term so that the challenge she gets is a challenge she can answer. The soundness proof is vacated when that happens, because it assumed a complete transcript and a complete transcript isn't what was built. A transcript missing one term is not a slightly weaker transcript. It is a different protocol wearing the name of the one that was proved sound.

Chapter 6 takes this up at Layer 5, in "Frozen Heart and What Came After." Trail of Bits disclosed the Frozen Heart vulnerability class in April 2022, affecting six independent implementations across three proof systems, and in each of them a public input or a commitment had been omitted from the Fiat-Shamir transcript, which let a malicious prover bias the challenge and forge proofs without knowing a valid witness [Trail of Bits, "Coordinated disclosure of vulnerabilities affecting Girault, Bulletproofs, and PLONK," April 2022]. Chapter 8 catalogs the incidents in forensic detail.

What was bought is a verifier that costs nothing and never sleeps and never has to be in the room, and can be re-run by a stranger in ten years against a file that has been sitting on a disk the whole time. What was paid is a new assumption that no theorem covers and a failure mode that doesn't look like a failure. At the table, a prover who cheated got caught out loud. She named her values, the line came up wrong, the round stopped, and the man across the table watched it happen. When a term goes missing from a hash, nothing stops. The proof still verifies. Every check passes and the file looks exactly like the file an honest prover holding a real witness would have produced, and the only thing wrong with it is that it's a lie.

It was a good bargain. It was still a bargain.

## The Phenomenon

The definitions you have been using are forty years old. Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff published them in 1985, and the work earned the first two of them the Turing Award. The game is not their paper. Their proof was about quadratic residues. The envelopes and the twelve lines are a teaching copy of the commit-and-open protocols that arrived the year after, when Oded Goldreich, Silvio Micali and Avi Wigderson showed the same trick working on ordinary combinatorial problems [GMW, STOC 1986]. Then for roughly twenty years all of it stayed almost entirely inside the literature. That wasn't neglect. The proofs of that era handled toy-sized problems, and the transformation that turned the event into an artifact was still a year off and would take two more decades to become practical.

The definitions have barely moved since. What moved was the size of the proofs, and then their price.

What Fiat and Shamir left behind was still large and still expensive to verify, and the algebraic work that went into that remainder was quiet. The structures got better and the security reductions got tighter. None of it made a newspaper.

Then in 2013 Rosario Gennaro, Craig Gentry, Bryan Parno and Mariana Raykova introduced Quadratic Arithmetic Programs, the first algebraic framework that made succinct non-interactive arguments practical [GGPR, Eurocrypt 2013; ePrint 2012/215]. In 2016 Jens Groth built on that lineage and produced the construction that hit hardware [Groth 2016; ePrint 2016/260]. QAPs were the precursor. Groth16 is the one people cite when they say 192 bytes.

Say which curve, though, because the slogan belongs to one and the gas bill belongs to another. A Groth16 proof is two points from one elliptic-curve group at forty-eight bytes each and one point from a second, larger companion group at ninety-six (pairing-friendly curves come as a matched pair, and the second group's points take twice the space), three group elements in total, compressed on the curve BLS12-381. That is the 192 bytes. It is less than a short tweet. It can certify a computation of millions of steps, every memory access checked and every constraint satisfied. Ethereum verifies Groth16 on a different curve, BN254, which is what its precompiles implement, and a verifier contract there costs roughly 250,000 gas, a few dollars of on-chain work. The gap between what gets proved and what gets sent is the whole reason anybody says succinct.

A STARK proof runs 50 to 200 kilobytes. That is hundreds of times larger, and at the top of the range a thousand times, and it is still small next to the computation behind it, because the size grows with the logarithm of the depth and not with the depth. A STARK needs no ceremony, and its security rests on hash functions and on the random-oracle idealization of them. The trade is real in both directions. You pay in bytes or you pay in a ceremony. Which of the two you want is the first real decision a designer makes, and Chapter 2 is where we make it.

But none of that explains why the world came looking. Three things did.

The first is what happens when verifying a fact requires assembling a dossier. In April 2024 a database scraped by a broker called National Public Data appeared on a forum called Breached, offered for sale at $3.5 million by an actor using the alias USDoD. The listing advertised 2.9 billion rows, which is far fewer people than it sounds like, since the rows repeat and overlap. What they carried was names, addresses and Social Security numbers. The breach had been running since late 2023 and the company didn't disclose it until August 2024 [Bleeping Computer, August 2024; the 2.9-billion figure is the hacker forum's own listing]. Nobody in those records had been asked. And you cannot change a Social Security number by walking into an office and asking for a new one.

The records existed because somebody needed to check things. When a regulator verifies that a bank's reserves cover its deposits, auditors go onsite for weeks and read individual account balances and transaction histories and counterparty relationships, and the bank's customers never consented to any of it. The regulators want more data. The banks want to protect their customers. Both sides are right, and both are stuck, because the only way anybody has ever verified the claim is to open the whole file to settle one bit of it. The file exists because somebody needed one bit out of it, and once it exists it can be stolen.

Regulation arrived on its own schedule. The European Parliament finalized eIDAS 2.0 as Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, published in April 2024, and it requires every member state to offer citizens a digital identity wallet capable of selective disclosure by 2026. Selective disclosure means proving your age, or your professional qualification, without handing over the document that carries it. For 450 million EU citizens that isn't a research agenda. It is law with a compliance deadline.

The second force has a price tag on it. Ethereum's base layer processes roughly 12 to 15 transactions per second. When demand runs past capacity, users bid against each other for block space. Fees spike to $50 or $100. A rollup moves the work off the main chain, so thousands of transactions get executed somewhere else and one proof comes back and Ethereum's validators verify that proof in a few milliseconds instead of re-running anything. For the execution it proved, the rollup inherits the base layer's security, because the proof is verified on the base layer, and it does not inherit the base layer's throughput limitation, because the computation happened elsewhere. L2Beat tracks roughly $47 billion across the whole L2 ecosystem [L2Beat, https://l2beat.com/scaling/tvs, retrieved early 2026]. Most of that sits behind optimistic rollups, which prove nothing and wait out a challenge window instead. The validity-proof share is the smaller one. It is also the share this book is about, and every dollar of it is a bet that the proof system works.

The third force is the price of a proof. The public prover tracker at ethproofs.org measured the same job across 2025 and watched the site-wide average fall from about $1.69 a proof in January to about four cents in December [Ethproofs, https://ethproofs.org, 2025 review]. That is a forty-two-fold collapse inside a single year, on one public method measuring one thing, and the modesty of that sentence is the point. What makes the number strange is where it came from. Nothing new had to be manufactured. The GPUs were already sitting in the racks; researchers found better algorithms and better ways to drive the hardware that was there. The hardware bill is still real, and Layer 5 is where this book charges it.

By late 2025 a production zkVM proved better than nine in ten Ethereum blocks inside the twelve-second slot, averaging near ten seconds on a GPU cluster [Succinct Labs, "SP1 Hypercube: Proving Ethereum in Real-Time," 2025]. The improvements compounded across the whole stack. Smaller fields cut the cost of each operation, better arithmetization cut the number of operations, and GPU parallelism cut the wall clock on whatever was left. The seven-layer model in this book is a map of where those cuts came from.

Then in December 2025 the Ethereum Foundation called the speed race won and named the next target, which is provable security at 128-bit strength by the end of 2026. On their reading the performance frontier is behind us. The security frontier is where we are standing.

At $1.69 a proof you prove financial settlements and nothing else. At four cents you stop asking whether you can afford to prove a thing. You start asking what you would have to believe for the proof to mean anything, and that question has seven answers.

## The Seven Layers at a Glance

This book takes a zero-knowledge system apart into seven layers. The cuts hold for the 319 rounds you played on paper and they hold for a rollup carrying a few billion dollars, and the two differ in the size of each layer rather than in the number of them. The layers behave more like organs than like floors in a building. Each one shapes what the others are able to do.

```
+----------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 1  SETUP            who built the parameters       |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 2  LANGUAGE         the circuit you write          |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 3  WITNESS          the secret you hold            |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 4  ARITHMETIZATION  the claim as polynomials       |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 5  PROOF SYSTEM     the certificate you send       |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 6  PRIMITIVES       the hard problems underneath   |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 7  VERIFICATION     who checks, and where          |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
```

Seven boxes carry the same shape. Each one holds a number, a name and a short tag. If you can redraw that on a napkin you can follow the rest of the book. What the picture gives you is a reading order and it is not a dependency order, and the boxes are empty until you fill them.

**Layer 1. The setup.** Before anybody proves anything, somebody has to build the mathematical parameters that the prover and the verifier will both use. The question that matters is who built them and whether you have to trust them. Ethereum's KZG Summoning of 2023 drew 141,416 participants over 208 days and each one contributed a share of a secret and then destroyed the share, and the whole arrangement holds if even one of those people was honest about the destroying [Ethereum Foundation, "Wrapping up the KZG Ceremony," January 2024]. You'll meet that ceremony in Chapter 2 and you'll ask what the parameters are worth if none of them told the truth. Nobody has ever proved that they were honest. Nobody could. There is an alternative, and a transparent setup skips the ceremony entirely. It pays for that in proof size and verification cost.

**Layer 2. The language.** You write the claim down in a programming language and the language decides which mistakes are available to you. It also decides what the compiler can catch on your behalf, which is less than you would like. The compiler is not on your side here. It will compile a circuit that proves the wrong thing and it won't warn you. One character has done real damage. Tornado Cash used `<--` where `<==` was needed, assignment where constrained assignment was required, and a value that should have been pinned down by the constraint system was merely computed instead. Chapter 3 tells that story. The bug is invisible if you read the source the way you read ordinary code.

**Layer 3. The witness.** You run the computation on your private data and you record every step of it, and nobody else ever sees the recording. That recording is the most underestimated bottleneck in the stack. It's also where privacy leaks in ways the mathematics above it never promised to cover. Tramèr, Boneh and Paterson timed Zcash's Groth16 prover and found the proving time correlated with the transaction amount closely enough to estimate the values the proof itself was hiding [USENIX Security 2020]. The proof was zero-knowledge. The machine generating it was not. Chapter 4 sits here.

The first three layers are preparation. You settle the parameters, you write the claim down, and you run the computation where nobody can watch. The next two turn that preparation into something a stranger can verify in milliseconds, and the last two supply the hard mathematics underneath and the public place where the check actually happens.

**Layer 4. The arithmetization.** The recording becomes polynomials, formulas built out of addition and multiplication like $3x^2 + 5x + 7$. Polynomials carry a great many constraints in very little space, and they have a property that makes a lie almost impossible to hide, because a wrong polynomial and a right one disagree at nearly every point and a randomly chosen point will almost certainly land on a disagreement. That single fact is why a proof can be verified in seconds when the computation behind it took hours. The 4x4 grid you played with becomes 72 polynomial constraints. Chapter 5 shows how the 72 become polynomials over a field with a million elements, and why testing them at one point drawn at random is enough.

**Layer 5. The proof system.** The constraint system gets compressed into one compact certificate that is sound and zero-knowledge at the same time, and this is where the two dominant families part company. Groth16 lives here. So do the STARKs, and so does every PLONK variant you'll meet later in the book. It's also where you learn how a 192-byte proof and a 200-kilobyte proof can both be called succinct, since succinct is a claim about growth rather than a claim about bytes.

**Layer 6. The primitives.** Underneath the proof system sit elliptic curves, hash functions and polynomial commitment schemes, which are methods for sealing a polynomial into an envelope that nobody can reopen quietly. Their security rests on three classes of hard problem. Those are discrete logarithms, hash collision resistance, and lattice problems, which are the post-quantum replacements already entering deployment. These are conjectures and not results. Nobody has proved that any of them is hard, and what we have instead is decades of clever people failing to break them. Chapter 7 covers the expiration date that quantum computers stamp on half of this and the race to have the replacements ready before it falls due.

**Layer 7. The verification.** Somebody verifies the proof somewhere public, in a smart contract or at a verifier endpoint, and the economics and the governance of that place decide whether the trick matters outside mathematics. Who can replace the verifier? Who pays for the check? In Chapter 8 you'll watch a governance attack drain a protocol whose code worked exactly as designed. The code did what it was written to do. That was the problem.

If you've ever wondered why proofs cost what they cost, or why a quantum computer threatens one proof system and leaves another alone, these seven layers are where the answers live. Each chapter that follows takes one of them, in the order the diagram gives.

But the dependencies do not follow the numbering, and that is the warning to carry forward. The primitive you pick at Layer 6 decides which proof systems are available at Layer 5. That decides which arithmetizations work at Layer 4. That shapes which languages are efficient at Layer 2, and that constrains the setup at Layer 1. One decision runs backwards through all seven. Choose a small fast field like Goldilocks and you have chosen against the pairing-friendly curves and everything they make possible, and the architecture reshapes itself around you. Some layers fuse outright. Jolt merges witness generation and arithmetization into a single lookup step. Cairo co-designs its language around its constraint system. In systems built that way, Layers 4, 5 and 6 behave as one inseparable design unit, which is why people call them the proof core. By Chapter 10 the model is redrawn in its honest form, a directed acyclic graph with fourteen causal edges rather than seven tidy floors.

The layers also let you ask the question that the word trustless is built to hide. At each layer, what are you actually trusting, and is there a reduction behind it? A reduction is an argument that breaking the system requires solving some particular mathematical problem, so that anybody who forges a proof has thereby done something the world has been failing to do for half a century. That isn't certainty. It is a transfer of the question onto a problem people have already attacked hard and failed to solve. But be exact about what that buys. It says that if somebody forges your proof, then somebody has also solved the discrete logarithm problem. It doesn't say the problem is unsolvable. It says the failure would be famous.

Three answers are possible and the table uses all three. Something is proved outright when the mathematics settles it with no assumption attached, and something is reduction-backed when its security rests on a named hard problem and there is an argument connecting the two. Everything else is procedure, which means a ceremony, a compiler, a machine or a committee. Run this audit yourself on any system somebody tries to sell you. Ask where the reduction is and what it reduces to, and then ask who is sitting in every other seat.

| Layer | a Groth16 SNARK | a STARK |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Setup | Procedural. A powers-of-tau ceremony plus a circuit-specific second phase. The guarantee is that one participant was honest and destroyed a share. No reduction. | Procedural, but nearly empty. Public randomness and no ceremony. What is left is the choice of hash and the derivation of its constants. No reduction. |
| 2. Language | Procedural. The circuit author and the compiler. No reduction. | Procedural, and identical. The same author, the same compiler. No reduction. |
| 3. Witness | Procedural. The witness generator, the host machine, the side channels. No reduction. | Procedural, unchanged. No reduction. |
| 4. Arithmetization | Split. The encoding step is proved outright, since a random point catches a wrong polynomial. Fidelity to what you meant is procedural, and that is the compiler again. | Split, and identical. Proved encoding, procedural fidelity, the compiler again. |
| 5. Proof system | Reduction-backed, to pairing assumptions on the curve, and argued in an idealized model of the group rather than against a falsifiable assumption. | Reduction-backed, to collision resistance of the hash, plus the random oracle model you met when the verifier was fired. |
| 6. Primitives | The assumption itself. Discrete logarithm and pairing assumptions, conjectures with reductions running up to them and nothing underneath. | The assumption itself. Collision resistance of a hash, and no discrete logarithm anywhere. |
| 7. Verification | Procedural. The verifier contract, whoever can replace it, the committee holding the keys. No reduction. | Procedural, the same committee problem. No reduction. |

Layer 4 is split down the middle, because the encoding step really is proved and the fidelity of the encoding really isn't, and no amount of mathematics at Layer 5 will ever notice that your constraint system describes a different puzzle than the one you had in mind. Layers 5 and 6 look alike on the page. They are not the same kind of thing. The reduction at Layer 5 says the proof system is secure if the primitive is. The assumption at Layer 6 is the primitive, and there's nothing further down to reduce it to. Three terms in the Groth16 row are new, and Chapter 7 defines all three. A pairing is an operation on an elliptic curve, and Groth16 is built on it. An idealized model of the group is the random-oracle move again, an assumption that the thing behaves perfectly rather than the way its code does, and a falsifiable assumption is one somebody could exhibit a counterexample to.

Two rows are not symmetric. Layers 1 and 6 are where the families differ in kind and not in degree. A Groth16 setup is a thing that particular people did on particular afternoons, and a transparent setup is a published constant that anybody can recompute. At Layer 6 the difference is the one Chapter 7 is about, since a quantum computer breaks discrete logarithms and leaves hash collision resistance standing, so one column carries an expiration date and the other does not. Everywhere else the two columns line up almost row for row, which is not how the argument between the families usually gets conducted.

Seven is a taxonomy and not a theorem. Nothing proves that seven is the right number, or that the cuts fall in the right places, and another author could draw six of them or nine and defend the choice. But read the table honestly and it says something uncomfortable. One layer out of seven is reduction-backed. One layer is the assumption itself, with nothing beneath it to reduce to. Half of one layer is proved outright. The remaining four and a half are procedure, which is to say a ceremony, a compiler, a machine or a committee. That arithmetic is the reason for the decomposition. The layers that are procedure are the layers where the incidents happen, and naming them separately is what lets you audit them separately and staff them separately and replace them one at a time without rebuilding everything around them. That last clause is the one this book has to earn: the layers couple in practice, and the chapters ahead show where. Trust-minimized is the accurate word, and this table is the sharpest form of it available.

Everything you did a few pages ago has grown into one of these layers. The grid she never showed you is a witness at Layer 3. The twelve lines are a constraint system at Layer 4. You, drawing one of them at random and checking what came back, are Layer 7, and the question of where that check runs now that you aren't in the room is the whole of Layer 7's trouble. Each of them has become a subfield with its own accidents. The game has not changed at all.

## The Stack on Disk

Seven layers stays a diagram until you look at a directory. Take Circom and snarkjs, which is a well-worn path to a Groth16 proof, and prove the same 4x4 grid with it. Eight files end up in the directory. The names are the standard ones with the circuit's name in front: `circuit.circom`, `circuit.r1cs`, `circuit.zkey` in the general case, and here the circuit is called sudoku. Each file belongs to a layer. Each one will hurt you in its own particular way if you trust it without looking.

You wrote one of them. You downloaded one from a stranger. A compiler wrote a third and a binding step wrote a fourth, and a witness generator wrote a fifth out of your private grid, which arrives in an input file this listing does not include, and the prover wrote two more after that. The eighth you exported by hand. Together they are most of the apparatus, sitting in a directory you could delete by accident.

Chapter 2 defines the Structured Reference String, the SRS, and Chapter 3 runs `snarkjs groth16 prove`. Both of those have an address on disk. These are the addresses.

**sudoku.circom** is Layer 2, the language. It is the only file here you wrote by hand. It holds sixteen cells and the rules for the rows and the columns and the boxes, in a language that looks enough like ordinary code to lull you into reading it that way. What goes wrong is the single character that cost Tornado Cash its soundness. The proof system will faithfully prove a false statement if the circuit asks it to. It holds no opinion about what you meant.

**sudoku.r1cs** is Layer 4, the arithmetization. It is what the compiler thinks you wrote, a rank-1 constraint system, the 72 constraints from a few pages back sitting in a binary file that no human reads. You can count the constraints in it. You cannot read them. Everything downstream works from this file and not from your source, so the proof proves the r1cs, and nothing between your source and the finished proof ever checks the constraint system against what you meant. If the compiler dropped a constraint, every proof you generate afterwards is valid and worthless.

**pot.ptau** is Layer 1, the setup. The name is short for powers of tau. This is the Structured Reference String from Chapter 2 with a filename on it, and it is the output of a ceremony of the same kind as the KZG Summoning, though not that ceremony, since a Groth16 circuit consumes a powers-of-tau file from a lineage of its own. It also fixes the curve, which on this path is BN254, the one Ethereum's precompiles implement. It is universal. It isn't tied to your circuit, and the same file serves everybody up to a size bound, which is why you almost certainly downloaded it from a URL rather than making it yourself. If the secret every participant promised to destroy survived anywhere, then everything derived from this file can be forged, and your proofs go with everybody else's. The file can be verified against the published ceremony transcript. Almost nobody does it, because the outcome is nearly always fine, which is the shape of every assumption that eventually costs somebody money.

**sudoku.zkey** is Layer 1 meeting Layer 5. It is the proving key, and it is made by binding the universal SRS in `pot.ptau` to your particular `sudoku.r1cs`. This is the one file where two layers touch, and the setup stops being universal here and becomes yours, and there is a second ceremony inside this step for Groth16, circuit-specific, that inherits every property of the first one. Accept a zkey from somebody else and you are proving their circuit, whatever your source file says. The binding happened before the file reached you and you can't see it by looking at it.

Here is the directory, listed in the order the files come into being rather than the order a shell would print them. Compiling the source makes the r1cs. The ptau arrives from outside. Binding the two makes the zkey. Then you hand your private grid to a witness generator, which reads it from an input file this listing does not include, and it writes the witness. Proving comes after that, and verifying comes last.

```
sudoku.circom
sudoku.r1cs
pot.ptau
sudoku.zkey
witness.wtns
proof.json
public.json
verification_key.json
```

**witness.wtns** is Layer 3, the witness. It holds the completed grid and every intermediate value the circuit computed on the way, written to disk in the clear. This is the secret. It sits in a build directory with ordinary file permissions and nothing about zero-knowledge protects it, because zero-knowledge is a property of the proof and not a property of your filesystem and the tidiest cryptography in the world will not stop a backup agent from copying this file to somebody else's server. The Zcash timing result lives at this layer too. The mathematics upstairs can be perfect while the machine underneath it leaks. Delete this file when you're done with it. Most build scripts don't.

Then `snarkjs groth16 prove` runs. It takes two inputs, `sudoku.zkey` and `witness.wtns`, and it brings two files into being.

**proof.json** is Layer 5, the sealed certificate. It holds three curve points on BN254, written out as decimal strings inside a JSON object. BN254's points are smaller than BLS12-381's, and the 192 bytes earlier in this chapter was the compressed figure on that larger curve. Uncompressed those points are about 256 bytes of actual proof, 128 if you compress them, and the file on disk is larger than either because JSON, so anybody who measures the file has measured the wrong number and will quote it in a slide deck. A proof also means nothing on its own. Handed to you by itself it is three numbers with no claim attached to them. A proof is an answer. Somebody still has to hold the question.

**public.json** is the claim those three numbers certify, and it is what the Layer 7 check reads alongside the proof. It holds the public inputs, which for this circuit means the seven given cells of the puzzle. That is a choice the circuit author made, since the source decides which signals are public and which stay in the witness. That is the question, written down where both parties can see it, and it is the only place the claim you wrote at Layer 2 resurfaces in a form a verifier can read. The proof is a statement about the contents of this file. It is a statement about nothing else in the world. Verify a proof against public inputs you did not choose yourself and you have proved nothing at all, because the prover picked the answer and the question both, and a valid proof of somebody else's question is worth what it cost you to check it.

**verification_key.json** is Layer 7's tool. It is exported from `sudoku.zkey` in a separate step and `snarkjs groth16 prove` does not produce it. A verifier needs three things (this key, the proof and the public inputs), and this is the one that doesn't arrive with the claim. It's the file that gets compiled into a smart contract and left there for years while everything around it changes. If it arrived from the same party that sent you the proof, verification is theater. They can hand you a key that accepts anything. The check will pass, and the passing will mean nothing.

There are checks you can run on all of this and few teams run any of them. You can verify the ptau against the published ceremony transcript. You can run `snarkjs zkey verify` against your r1cs and your ptau, which is the one check that tests whether the proving key really was built from the circuit and the ceremony you think it was. You can inspect the r1cs for its constraint count and compare that against what a circuit of this shape ought to produce. Re-export the verification key from a zkey you built yourself and compare it byte for byte against the one somebody handed you. Verify the proof locally, against public inputs you wrote down first, before it ever reaches a contract. None of that is difficult. But the happy path is seductive, and a green check appears whether or not anybody looked, and the green check looks the same either way.

The directory is missing three things. Nothing here holds the puzzle's rules as you understood them, and nothing records who gave you the ptau or which contract the verification key was deployed to. Those three facts decide whether the eight files mean anything at all. None of the three is written down anywhere on the machine. They live in a design document, or in somebody's memory, or in a deployment script that nobody has read in a year. The eight files are the part of the system a computer can verify, and the part a computer cannot verify is where the losses come from.

That is one file you wrote, one you downloaded, five the machines made out of those two and your private grid, and one you exported by hand. Nearly every one of them has a layer of its own, and `sudoku.zkey` has two, which is the most interesting fact in the directory. Every file in the listing is one piece of the game, grown up, and the grid she never showed you is now a file with ordinary permissions in a build directory. Your draw from the twelve lines is now a verification key in a contract that somebody else can replace.

## The Deepest Question

Seven layers means seven seats. Somebody sits in each one, or a named mathematical assumption does, and the system holds only as long as that occupant behaves. Whether the trust is there is not an interesting question. It is there. The interesting question is what it would cost to evict each occupant and hire a replacement without pulling the building down. Three kinds of bill come back. Some seats cost money, and you can pay for those with a purchase order. Some cost coordination, which means getting a large number of people who do not work for you to do the same thing in the same year. And some cost the pain of moving, which is the work of tearing out one part and finding that four others came away with it. At some layers nobody has ever published a number. Where that is true, this section says so rather than guessing.

At Layer 1 the seat belongs to the 141,416 people who ran the KZG Summoning. The parameters are good if at least one of those contributions was destroyed and never recovered. One, out of 141,416. That is the entire threshold, and nobody can check it, then or now. If it fails, a forger can mint unlimited fraudulent proofs on every system built on those parameters, and tens of billions of dollars in ZK rollups rest on the word. Chapter 2 makes it precise. You cannot fire 141,416 people. You can run a second ceremony, which took 208 days the last time and needs a fresh crowd and has no published cost sheet anywhere, so the bill is coordination and nothing but coordination. Or you can evict the ceremony altogether by moving to a transparent setup, and that bill is paid in proof size and verification expense on every proof the system will ever check, for as long as it runs. The second option does not replace an occupant. It replaces the proof system, and Layers 4, 5 and 6 come away with it.

At Layer 2 the occupant is a person and there is no mathematics to hide behind. Someone writes the circuit, and the bill that seat sends is the pain of moving. Chaliasos and colleagues catalogued 141 SNARK vulnerabilities and found 95 of them, about two-thirds, were circuit-layer soundness bugs, with under-constrained circuits the largest single class [Chaliasos et al., "SoK: What Don't We Know? Understanding Security Vulnerabilities in SNARKs," USENIX Security 2024]. That figure belongs to a seam rather than to a floor. You write the circuit at Layer 2 and a compiler turns it into a constraint system at Layer 4, and the bug lives in the gap between what you wrote and what the constraints actually pin down. The most sophisticated proof system in the world will faithfully prove a false statement if the circuit asks it to, and it will be perfectly sound while it does so, because it is proving the relation you encoded and not the one you meant. The character you met a few pages ago let Tornado Cash withdraw funds that were never deposited, and the protocol held around $650 million at its 2022 peak. Chapter 3 tells that story. This occupant cannot be evicted, because the seat has to be filled by somebody. What you can buy is checking, and nobody has published what checking costs. Rewriting a circuit in a safer language means auditing it again at Layer 2, and generating a new constraint system at Layer 4, and generating a new circuit-specific proving key, which sends you back to the ceremony at Layer 1, and deploying a new verifier contract at Layer 7. One bad character costs four layers to remove.

At Layer 3 the seat belongs to whoever owns the machine that computes the witness, and the Zcash timing result is what that seat looks like when it is occupied badly. The proof is zero-knowledge. The act of making it may not be. Privacy here is partly a luxury good. Client-side proving needs roughly 4 GB of RAM and ten seconds or more on a modern phone, and it maximizes privacy and stays out of reach of most users, while delegated proving is available to everyone and requires trusting someone else with your secrets. The people with the most to hide have the least ability to hide it. Chapter 4 confronts that asymmetry. Evicting the delegated prover costs money, and the money is not raised anywhere central. It is paid one handset at a time, by every user who has to carry the 4 GB and the ten seconds themselves, and that is why this occupant keeps getting rehired. Midnight's compiler enforces privacy boundaries at compile time, and it is one response to the problem. A disclosure belongs here: this book's author founded Input Output Global, the company that built Midnight, and every claim about it in Chapter 12 is cited to public documentation or to measured behavior so that a reader who distrusts the source can check the work. That chapter also shows that compile-time guarantees and runtime privacy are two different problems.

At Layer 4 nobody sits in the seat. A claim occupies it, and the bill this layer sends is the pain of moving. The claim is that the constraint system is satisfied if and only if the computation was performed correctly, and it is not automatic. An under-constrained system is one whose equations can be satisfied by a witness that corresponds to no correct execution at all. The proof verifies. The computation never happened. That is the far side of the seam you read about at Layer 2, the same bug arriving from the other direction, and it is what the catalogued vulnerabilities were mostly counting. The other half of Layer 4's story is what the encoding costs. Consider a lending rule. "If the balance exceeds the threshold, approve the loan" is a comparison, free enough on a laptop that nobody measures it. Arithmetized it becomes a range check, which is a proof that a number lies between two bounds, written with nothing but addition and multiplication. Every value in the system lives in a finite field, a fixed set of numbers you can add and multiply inside without ever leaving it, and the size of that set fixes the price of the range check. Two field sizes compete for the job. Goldilocks is the small one, 64 bits wide, $2^{64} - 2^{32} + 1$. The large one is the field that comes attached to a 254-bit elliptic curve. Goldilocks makes every individual operation cheap, and it cannot hold a number wider than 64 bits, so a wide range check has to be split across several field elements and the constraint count goes up. The large field costs more for every operation and swallows the whole value in one element. Neither one wins. The number you measured changes with the field, and it does not travel with you. Replacing an arithmetization means choosing a different field, and the field determines the primitives underneath it and the proof system above it, so the constraint count you measured is gone and so is the engineering built on top of it. Nobody has published what it costs to rewrite a production constraint system, and a number invented here would be worth less to you than the admission that there is not one. Chapter 5 does the encoding in full.

At Layer 5 the occupant is the proof system and the security reduction standing behind it, and a reduction is a proof about a specification rather than a proof about the code that ships. Frozen Heart is that gap made visible, six independent implementations and one omitted term apiece [Trail of Bits, April 2022]. The mathematics was sound. The soundness proof was vacated anyway. Chapter 6 works the transcript in detail, and Chapter 8 catalogs what has been forged since. Layer 5 also sends an invoice every month. Measured end-to-end across real workloads the overhead runs 10,000x to 50,000x, four to five orders of magnitude between running a computation and proving that you ran it, and Chapter 5 projects a plausible floor of 1,000x to 5,000x for well-engineered systems. Today the tax is why proving a single Ethereum block inside its slot takes a cluster of GPUs rather than a machine. Those GPUs are the standing rent for keeping the occupant, and the rent is money, paid continuously, in hardware and in the power bill behind it. Evicting the occupant is charged in a different currency. A new proof system needs new keys from Layer 1 and a new verifier at Layer 7, so the bill is dominated by what the swap drags along, and that is the pain of moving. Chapters 5 and 6 show where the tax comes from, why it is falling, and whether it has a floor.

At Layer 6 the occupants have names and none of them is a person. They are the hardness of the discrete logarithm in an elliptic-curve group, and the collision resistance of a hash function, and the hardness of lattice problems. These are conjectures and not theorems, and nobody has proved that the problems cannot be solved efficiently, and the confidence in them comes from decades of failing to break them. BN254 is widely deployed and has already seen its security erode from an estimated 128 bits to roughly 100. Chapter 7 explains the race. There are two bills here and they get confused with each other. Replacing the curve is the cheaper one. BLS12-381 is larger and stronger and available today, and moving to it is the pain of moving and has been done before by teams who lived to describe it. Replacing the assumption is the other bill. NIST's transition timeline retires the quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms by 2035, so every ZK system standing on elliptic curves carries an implicit expiration date. Hash functions are not on that list, which is why the hash-based systems in this book are already on the far side of the date. For everybody else, getting out from under it means a different family of assumption and not a bigger curve. This is the one bill with a due date printed on it and no price yet. The replacements are still arriving.

At Layer 7 the seat is a committee. Most ZK rollups sit at Stage 0 or Stage 1 on L2Beat's maturity scale, a community-maintained trust ranking for rollups, which means a small multisig can replace the verifier contract. Six layers of mathematical elegance, and the seventh is a handful of people holding keys. What such a committee is capable of is not a ZK question, and the clearest illustration on record is not a ZK system at all. Beanstalk lost $182 million in a single 13-second transaction when an attacker used a flash loan to buy enough governance tokens to pass a malicious emergency proposal and drain the treasury [Immunefi, "Hack Analysis: Beanstalk Governance Attack, April 2022"; PeckShield post-mortem]. The books recorded $182M destroyed; PeckShield estimated the attacker netted around $80M after flash-loan repayments, with the balance burned or stranded. Beanstalk had no circuits and no proofs and none of the seven layers under it. The governance mechanism worked exactly as designed. That is the lesson, and it is a lesson about what a committee does to any stack it sits on top of, which is every stack in this book. Chapter 8 does the forensics, on Beanstalk and on the ZK verifier incidents that belong to this layer properly. The bill here reads strangely against the other six, because burning the keys costs nothing in money and could be done tomorrow afternoon, and what the money buys you is a system nobody can ever patch again. So the price is coordination, and it is the hardest coordination in the stack, because it is paid by a committee voting to abolish itself.

No single layer requires trusting one entity with everything. Each assumption can be falsified and audited and, in principle, replaced without touching the other six. In practice the bills above keep landing at layers that did not order them, and that coupling is what makes replacement hard, and the tension between the principle and the practice is the spine of the next thirteen chapters.

Layer 1 and Layer 7 are human decisions about who holds power. Layers 4, 5 and 6 are mathematics. Layers 2 and 3 are neither, and the table already said so: a compiler and a machine in a room, procedure both. The system converts human trust into mathematical certainty and then converts that certainty back into human trust. A ceremony with 141,416 participants and a multisig holding a verifier contract are both questions about honesty, and they are not the same question. The ceremony needs one honest contribution out of many, once, and then it is finished. The multisig needs a threshold of its keyholders to stay honest and available at the same time, every day, for as long as the contract is live. One of those thresholds gets easier as the crowd grows. The other does not.

## The First Decision

You have already played this game. Three hundred and nineteen lines drawn at random, three hundred and nineteen tables of sixteen envelopes, and at the end of it a number rather than a certainty: if she was bluffing, she reached the end with a chance under 2^-40. That grid is not finished with you. It is the running example for the next four chapters, and Chapters 3 through 6 take the same sixteen cells and run them through a factory.

First the puzzle becomes a program, sixteen variables and a handful of rules, written in a language the proof system can read. Chapter 3 does that, at Layer 2. Then it becomes a witness, the completed grid that only the prover sees, with every intermediate step recorded. Chapter 4 shows at Layer 3 why that recording is the most underestimated bottleneck in the stack. After that come 72 polynomial constraints, every row holding {1, 2, 3, 4} with no repeats, encoded over a million-element field where a single check at a random point catches a wrong answer. That is Layer 4, and the work of Chapter 5. Last it becomes a proof, a handful of numbers smaller than the sentence you are reading, and those numbers convince a stranger that the solution exists. Chapter 6 seals it.

Each transformation strips away one more layer of human readability and puts mathematical certainty in its place. By the end the puzzle will be unrecognizable. That is what the machinery is for.

Whatever path you take through the rest of this, do not skip Chapter 2 or the trust decomposition in Chapter 10. The first is where the deepest trust decisions live. The second is the thesis in its most complete form, seven layers and fourteen causal edges and three architectural paths, each with a different failure profile. The Glossary at the front of the book is the quick-reference companion for any term a later chapter introduces without warning.

The field is crossing three frontiers, and the order is a reading rather than a schedule. Performance is largely crossed. The forty-two-fold fall in the price of a proof across 2025 and the arrival of block proving inside the slot are the evidence for it. Security is the frontier now. It means formal verification and 128-bit provable security and post-quantum readiness. Privacy is the one after. It means compiler-enforced disclosure boundaries and constant-time implementations. Where you enter this story depends on which of the three matters most to your work.

Before the puzzle can become any of those things, the stage has to be built. Building the stage means creating the mathematical parameters that the prover and the verifier will both use, and it happens before a single proof exists, and the important question about it is not how it is built but who builds it and whether you have to trust them.

The ceremonies scaled from intimate to planetary in seven years. The Zcash Sprout ceremony in 2016 had six participants, each generating a share of the secret parameters and then destroying the share, and the system was secure if even one of the six was honest [Zcash Foundation, "The Design of the Ceremony," 2016]. Two years later the Zcash Sapling ceremony used the BGM17 multi-party protocol and grew to roughly ninety contributors. By 2023 the KZG Summoning had taken the same principle to 141,416. A transparent setup sidesteps the whole arrangement, because it needs no ceremony and no participants and no destroyed shares, and it pays for that freedom in proof size and verification expense. Trusted setup or transparent setup. That is Chapter 2, and it is the first fork in the road, and its consequences echo through every layer after it.

The old arrangement gave you one seat and no bill you were allowed to read, and this one gives you seven seats and seven bills, and where a bill has no number it says so. None of that is trustless. It is trust divided and priced and open to argument, which is a smaller promise than the marketing makes and the only one anybody can keep. Seven bills you can read beat one you cannot.
